Methodology

The rules that stand between a population of market events and a claim about it. Most of them exist to stop a surface looking larger, cleaner or more independent than it is.

What is public here
The methods, described as concepts. No formula, threshold, parameter name or measured value appears on this page.

01 Episode collapse

Anchor count is not observation count.

Several event anchors can sample one underlying market move. Anchor count is therefore not observation count, and results are collapsed to independent episodes before anything is claimed.

This single step usually shrinks a surface more than any other, which is exactly why it comes first rather than last.

02 Duplicate inflation

The gap between what you counted and what you have.

The ratio between raw anchors and independent episodes. It grows with holding length, so the same surface can look far larger than it is.

Because the inflation is a function of how long a position is held, two studies of the same events are not comparable unless this is stated for both.

03 Path duplication fingerprints

Two ways to catch one move wearing many labels.

Two diagnostics detect anchors sampling one move: the share of anchors sharing a peak timestamp, and the share sharing an identical give-back value.

Both are cheap and both are checked, because an identical value arrived at twice is almost never a coincidence — it is the same path counted twice.

04 Paired-within-episode design

Compare inside the episode, not across it.

When two arms occupy the same episode they ride the same price path, so they are compared pairwise inside the episode. This removes the shared market motion that otherwise dominates.

Comparing across episodes measures mostly the market. Comparing within one measures the difference between the arms, which is the only thing the question was ever about.

05 Oracle / causal separation

A label built from hindsight may measure, never decide.

A label computed from the realised path may be used to measure how badly something is timed, but never to build a rule. Rules may only use information available at or before their own timestamp.

The two are kept apart by construction rather than by discipline, because the failure mode here is silent: a rule that quietly borrows an oracle label looks excellent and means nothing.

06 Reversal lifecycle

Six transitions, six separate questions.

A pressure event is described as a sequence: pressure, persistence, exhaustion, reversal, plateau, deterioration. Each transition is a separate observable question.

Treating the whole sequence as one event hides which transition an idea actually depends on — and therefore which one would refute it.

07 Reaction window

Size is irrelevant if it does not hold.

How long a favourable phase holds before it decays determines whether any rule can act on it at all. A phase that decays immediately cannot be traded regardless of its size.

So the question asked of a phase is not how large it is but how long it stays — and the second question can refuse a candidate the first would have accepted.

08 Resolution tiers

Every episode is analysed at the resolution it actually has.

Every episode is classified by the finest data that actually exists for it — tick, second, minute, or ledger-path only. Analyses are restricted to the tier they can honestly support.

The classification is per episode rather than per study, because coverage is uneven and a single study will usually span more than one tier.

09 Live ledger timestamping

A statistic is only true of the record it was computed against.

Forward records grow continuously, so every statistic carries the state of the record it was computed against and is re-verified before being quoted.

Without that stamp, two numbers taken a day apart look like a disagreement when they are simply answers to different questions.
